tldr: The morning's question, does sight change what a standing fly does, is answered end to end by the afternoon. The eye's motion detector fires with the right direction on the champion's own settings; its left-right signal reaches the HS steering cells through the connectome and vanishes when the detector is off; and there it stops. A steering command injected straight into DNa02, the turn neuron, at the strength the eye delivers and at five times that, moves the leg motor neurons' left-right balance by under half a hertz. A walking command volley reaches the rhythm-generating interneurons within 35 to 55 ms and the motor neurons by 1.6 Hz; the command neurons themselves fire at 72 Hz with the command off, driven by the cord. The nerve cord walks on its own recurrence and does not take steering or start-stop commands from the brain at the champion's balance. One measured fact explains three open threads: the null on sight, the walk command's non-monotone dose, the stop neuron that cannot stop it. It is the wall every brain-borne behaviour will meet, and sight was the first to measure it station by station.

The graph agrees with the dynamics (lane B, 15:05, static): BANC's DNa02 makes
no synapse onto the 24 Pugliese CPG types the walk command reaches; it makes
382 and 328 excitatory synapses onto ipsilateral leg motor neurons, none
across the midline. The CPG types load the motor neurons at near-equal
excitation and inhibition (4,659 against 4,876 synapses), which is why a walk
command volley moves them +15 Hz and the motor neurons +1.6 (lane B, 14:35).

## What the cord does instead

With the walking command switched off, the two DNg100 command cells still fire
at 71.8 Hz, driven by the cord's own recurrence; the overlay takes them to 137.
The command-off fly walks 6.8 mm in six seconds; the published dose shape (0,
40 and 94 Hz) gives 6.8, 8.3 and 5.6 mm, not monotone in drive; DNg93, the
stop neuron, does not stop it (the judge's start-stop reads earlier this week).
The cord generates its own walk and its own turning, about 100 degrees of drift
per six seconds, and the brain's commands are modulations of a few percent on
top of it. That is the champion; a real fly starts, stops and steers on
descending command.

## Why this is the day's result and not a failure

The zero-conflicts aim says a conflict found is a region of parameter space
deleted. This one deletes a large region: no gain, frame, contrast or trial
length on the stimulus side will turn this fly, because the signal is already
in the steering cells and the cord does not act on descending asymmetry. Lane
C stopped at exactly that point, on the shift rule, after testing the endpoint
twice at its shortest fair form; lane B measured the remaining stations in
three packets over an hour. The question that replaces "does sight reach the
fly" is a mechanism question with an owner: what carries a descending steering
command to the motor side balance in the animal, and which part of the model's
copy is inert (`gap.descending_steering_inert_in_cord`, lane B, static
downstream reads of DNa02 and DNg100 by target class on both animals against
the balance scale the model applies to each class).
