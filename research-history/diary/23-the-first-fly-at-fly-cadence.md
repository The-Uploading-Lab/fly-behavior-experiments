The throne under bar v3.1 had been vacant since the bar was written.
Two flies had cleared v3 on a battery and failed the confirmation;
the bar answered by demanding two independent batteries at measured
input statistics, and for three days nothing met it.

r23 met it four times.

The story of how starts with a defeat. Lane A spent the evening
proving that the bar's own two clauses — stand often, step at fly
cadence — trade off against each other across every controller ever
built: correlation −0.675, no controller in both clauses, twenty-nine
tried. A bar whose clauses oppose each other is not impossible, just
narrow: the champion has to live on a ridge. The lane then changed how
it searches — re-rank the shortlist on held-out seeds instead of
trusting the chamber's maximum — and the candidate that came through
was the one ranked third. The re-ranking's own top pick failed both
its batteries. The ridge is real.

Then the judging nearly went wrong, in a way worth recording forever.
The judge's first confirmation battery came back 4 of 12 — a clear
failure — and for a few hours the record read three lane passes, one
lane failure, one judge failure. But a champion's row from the lane's
own battery would not reproduce on the judge's rig: same parameters,
same seed, a standing fly on theirs, a fallen one on the judge's. The
cause was not the fly. The judge's battery instrument had never
learned to read `adh_gain` — the foot-adhesion dial, a ledgered
mechanism for three days — so it silently ran the champion with the
wrong feet. The fix took ten lines and a bit-identity probe; the
re-run battery stood 9 of 12, stepping at 5.18 Hz, inside the fly band.
The renderer had the mirror gap with the afferent-regularity dial, and
its parity gate refused the first champion clip at 3.98 mm against a
recorded 4.05 — the gate doing precisely what it was built for.

So the crown rests on four honest batteries out of five, all at
measured input regularity, with the one failure printed on the card
beside the passes. The champion's cadence clears the band's edge by
one to five grid steps. Its true standing rate is about 0.65 against a
clause that needs 0.54. It is a marginal champion, crowned by a rule
that asks for replication and got it, and every number that argues
against it is published in its own record.

And it is lost. The same night, the lane's own audit found that thirty
results about the fly's heading had been measured through an estimator
that returns confident answers for pure noise — the fly never
spiralled, never turned; its body yaw drifts about one degree per run.
The results were withdrawn in a block, unedited, and the correction
measured properly: the champion walks at fly speed along its path,
holds its heading like a compass, and its center of mass simply moves
with no preferred direction — backward almost exactly as often as
forward. Path speed 9.1 mm/s, inside the real fly's band. Net speed
0.457, twenty times short.

The first fly at fly cadence steps beautifully, faces forward, and
arrives nowhere. What is missing is no longer a mystery of mechanics —
nineteen proposed mechanisms are dead — but, the connectome accounting
now says, mostly an absence: the heading-integration circuitry is 0.4%
present in the model's slice of the brain, and the largest missing
input to the descending layer is vision, twenty-six times larger than
the compass. The throne is filled. The question it was built to force
is finally clean: not "why does the fly fall" and not "why is it
slow," but "what does it take for a fly that walks to go somewhere."
